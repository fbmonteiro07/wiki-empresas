If the West wants to win today’s technology competition, it should first revisit what happened to global manufacturing after China entered the World Trade Organization in 2001.

The conventional narrative is that China climbed the value chain because it gradually mastered more advanced technology.

I think the sequence was, to a significant extent, the reverse.

China first captured the mass market by producing goods that were substantially cheaper and “good enough” for most customers, often supported by massive state subsidies and industrial policy.

Scale then generated cash flow, engineering talent, supplier networks, manufacturing know-how and continuous learning. Those advantages enabled Chinese companies to move steadily up the value chain.

Mass market → Scale → Capability → Higher-value products → Technological leadership

After WTO accession, China displaced competitors across one industry after another. It began with textiles, garments, toys and furniture before moving into consumer electronics, telecommunications equipment, solar panels, batteries, electric vehicles, drones and advanced industrial machinery.

China did not become a technological power because it first achieved technological leadership.

It acquired much of that capability because it first achieved manufacturing scale.

That history increasingly resembles what we are seeing across technology today.

Western companies still lead at the frontier, producing many of the most important breakthroughs.

Chinese companies, meanwhile, are rapidly improving while driving costs sharply down.

For most customers, they do not need to offer the best product.

They only need to offer one that is good enough at a much lower price.

Whether in AI models, robotics, electric vehicles or industrial technology, a product delivering 75–80% of the performance at a fraction of the cost can capture an enormous market. Once enough customers adopt it, revenues grow, ecosystems develop, engineering capability improves and the frontier becomes progressively easier to challenge.

The lesson for the West is equally clear: frontier leadership rests on a broad industrial base. Manufacturing showed what happens when that base erodes. Lose enough of it, and eventually you lose the frontier as well.

The West therefore needs to protect the entire pyramid.

The objective is to preserve the industrial base that makes the next generation of technological leadership possible.

Lose the base of the pyramid, and eventually you lose the summit.

I believe greater market protection and trade frictions are inevitable when competing against a mercantilist system.

The bigger challenge for Western governments is to come to terms with the consequences of allowing so much of their manufacturing base to migrate to China under a largely laissez-faire philosophy that assumed markets alone would produce the right outcome.

That assumption proved costly in manufacturing.

The West cannot afford to make the same mistake in technology.

Samsung Electronics has delayed the mass production schedule for its CXL (Compute eXpress Link) 3.1 based memory module (CMM-D 3.0). The delay follows the postponed launches of CXL 3.1 supporting central processing units (CPUs) from Intel, AMD and others.

According to industry sources on the 6th, Samsung Electronics will maintain production of its CXL 2.0 based memory module CMM-D 2.0 for the time being. It had originally planned to pursue customer sample production of CMM-D 3.0 this past June, but that schedule has been pushed back.

An industry official said, "The transition to additional CXL 2.0 mass production aligned with demand was delayed, and the market has been slow to open because the CXL 3.1 ecosystem is not yet built out." The official added, "CXL 3.1 production in the fourth quarter of this year will be geared strongly toward sample testing purposes." Full scale mass production is expected next year.

CXL is an interconnect technology based on the PCI Express (PCIe) interface. While the data transfer protocol differs, the physical connection terminal and data transfer bandwidth performance are identical. CXL 3.1 uses PCIe 6.0, while CXL 2.0 uses PCIe 5.0.

To realize PCIe 6.0 performance, components that support the interface are essential. These include server CPUs, graphics processing units (GPUs), solid state drives (SSDs) and motherboards. Currently, most PCIe 6.0 supporting components remain unreleased.

An industry official explained, "Neither Intel nor AMD has released a PCIe 6.0 supporting CPU," adding, "Next generation platforms such as Intel Diamond Rapids and AMD EPYC Venice must be supplied before the server industry can adopt CXL 3.1." The launch of Intel's server CPU Diamond Rapids was targeted for the second half of this year but has been delayed to the second to third quarter of next year. This is reportedly due to a combination of design changes and production yield issues.

AMD's EPYC Venice launch is set for the second half of this year. Engineering samples (ES) have been supplied to major vendors. Samsung Electronics will produce CMM-D 3.1 samples validated on EPYC Venice based systems from September onward. Mass production will begin after the samples pass customer quality testing. The industry sees the earliest feasible mass production timing as the first quarter of next year.

Samsung Electronics' initial plan called for a sample unveiling late last year. Song Taeksang, vice president at Samsung Electronics, stated at the Open Compute Project (OCP) Global Summit in San Jose, United States this past October that the company would introduce the industry's first CMM-D product supporting the CXL 3.1 standard in the fourth quarter of 2025.

Interest in the CXL market has declined as demand concentrated on commodity DRAM modules and high bandwidth memory (HBM). Combined with the delayed buildout of the CXL 3.1 ecosystem, this is seen as the reason Samsung Electronics postponed its mass production plan.

SK Hynix and Micron have also completed research and development (R&D) of CXL 3.1 based memory modules, but no signs of production preparation have been detected.

CXL memory supports a memory pooling function. Using switch technology, memory is partitioned and shared, so the scope of memory utilization is not tied to the CPU or GPU. By reducing the memory capacity that each processing unit must carry onboard, it improves efficiency.

Memory capacity expansion is also flexible. It connects to a terminal much like a PC SSD. Conventional server DRAM memory modules (RDIMM) could only be mounted in dedicated slots. Compared with HBM, CXL memory offers lower bandwidth and operating speed, making the two complementary.

Samsung Electronics responded, "There have been some changes to certain plans, but we are preparing the previously planned mass production schedule without disruption." This is interpreted to mean mass production remains possible within the fourth quarter of this year.
