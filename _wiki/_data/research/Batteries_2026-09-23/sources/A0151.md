Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy's criticism of Ford's ties to Chinese entities creates a new regulatory overhang for Ford, but we think it's likely more benign than feared. 
Key takeaways	 

*	Sec. Duffy’s criticism does not block CATL technology use or tax credits, but we see added regulatory overhang for Ford’s ESS business. 
*	We believe Ford engaged extensively with the Trump administration to confirm CATL licensing compliance, raising questions about the timing of this pushback. 
*	This dispute highlights an uncomfortable reality: unprecedented power demand lifts the need for US battery storage manufacturing, but know-how sits in China. 
*	Will policymakers and the domestic manufacturing base be able to find common ground? 

Transportation Secretary, Sean Duffy, sent a letter to Ford <[original URL omitted; available in raw archive] , criticizing its ties to Chinese entities, including its licensing agreement with CATL (covered by Jack Lu). This does not, by itself, prevent Ford from using CATL technology or change Ford's tax-credit eligibility. However, we believe it creates an additional regulatory overhang for Ford's ESS business while the company navigates discussions with the administration. Sec. Duffy specifically criticized Ford's continued reliance on CATL's licensed technology and technical expertise, arguing that it runs counter to US objectives around supply-chain independence and technological self-reliance.

Based on our discussions with the company, we believe Ford's management team has had extensive engagement with the Trump administration regarding its CATL licensing structure to ensure that it complies with the applicable rules and regulations – raising the question: why is the company now receiving pushback? Ford strongly disputed <[original URL omitted; available in raw archive] the characterization of the relationship, emphasizing that the CATL arrangement is a limited technology licensing and services agreement rather than a joint venture or foreign-owned manufacturing operation. Ford has consistently maintained that it owns the plant, controls production and employs the workforce – a structure the company has described publicly since the agreement was first announced.

The mechanism through which the administration could ultimately restrict the Ford/CATL licensing agreement remains unclear. Sec. Duffy's letter does not direct Ford to terminate the agreement or cite a specific statutory authority that would prohibit it. That said, the administration could explore avenues through which it could make the arrangement less economically attractive, including the application of prohibited foreign entity rules and the associated clean-energy tax credits. Treasury's framework specifically examines whether contractual arrangements with a specified foreign entity confer "effective control," making the precise structure of Ford's licensing agreement important.

More broadly, we believe the dispute exposes an uncomfortable reality in US industrial/energy policy. The US is entering a period of unprecedented power demand growth, increasing the need for domestic manufacturing of critical energy infrastructure, including battery storage. Yet, a substantial share of the global battery supply chain, manufacturing base and technological know-how resides in China. Our prior work <[original URL omitted; available in raw archive]  argued that Ford's ability to combine CATL's proven LFP technology with US-owned manufacturing was precisely what could allow it to emerge as a differentiated domestic ESS supplier.

So what now? Does the US pursue increasingly restrictive rules around Chinese technology licensing, potentially slowing the build-out of domestic battery manufacturing at a time when power availability is becoming a constraint on AI infrastructure? Or do policymakers accept some level of pragmatic technology transfer, provided that the physical assets, workforce, supply chain and operating control remain in the hands of US companies?
