Market commentary, not research. 

CATL has shed US$78bn in market value, with US$58bn evaporating since 18 August alone. 

A relentless barrage of ESS concerns, negative headlines, and an absence of a credible turnaround narrative has turned one of Asia's most crowded longs into one of the market's favourite shorts. The stock's collapse is obvious. 

The more important question is whether the bear case is now fully priced in, or if this is merely the beginning of a deeper de-rating.

CATL: FROM LONG-ONLY DARLING TO HEDGE FUND CONSENSUS SHORT:

CATL - What’s Driving the Sell-Off?

The market is searching for reasons to stay bearish on CATL, and in the current tape, every rumour has become a headline.

The latest concern centres on September production schedules, with market chatter suggesting battery production growth remains positive month-on-month but is falling short of bullish expectations. There are also reports that CATL is pressuring suppliers on pricing, fuelling concerns that underlying demand may not be as robust as previously assumed. While these claims are difficult to verify, they have reinforced fears of a slowing demand environment. 

Against this backdrop, management's message remains unchanged: utilization rates are running at full capacity, orders remain strong, and historically CATL has delivered on similar assertions.

The second overhang is profitability. 

Fresh market rumours point to 3Q earnings and unit profit expectations being revised lower. 

CATL has never guided absolute profit levels and has consistently maintained that unit economics should remain stable over the longer term. 

However, in a stock under pressure, investors tend to trade the first headline they hear and ask questions later. Weak sentiment is amplifying what would otherwise be routine earnings speculation.

Competition is another recurring bear argument. OEMs continue to pursue battery self-sufficiency, either through in-house development or partnerships with second-tier suppliers. 

While this narrative has resurfaced with intensity, the reality is that it is hardly new. CATL's domestic market share has gradually normalized from roughly 50% six years ago to around 45% today, but this has occurred alongside a battery market that has expanded more than ninefold. CATL remains the dominant player in both scale and technology. Market share dilution in a rapidly growing industry is very different from structural share loss.

CATL’s competitive moat also remains intact. 

Battery manufacturing is a scale business requiring enormous capital investment, advanced technology, and operational expertise. CATL continues to lead on quality, cost competitiveness, balance-sheet strength, and profitability. While further domestic share gains may be difficult, maintaining a market share north of 40% remains entirely achievable. More importantly, overseas the company continues to take share, particularly in Europe, where it is steadily displacing Korean competitors.

Buybacks have become another source of investor frustration. 

CATL announced a sizeable RMB20-40bn repurchase and cancellation programme alongside interim results in late July, yet remained largely absent from the market as the share price weakened. The company finally executed its first buyback on September 11, but at only RMB200m, the signal was underwhelming relative to expectations. Investors naturally ask: if management is not aggressively buying at these levels, why should anyone else?

Several additional headlines have attracted attention but appear less material. News of scrutiny around the Hungary plant and China's tighter approval process for new battery capacity have generated noise, but neither alters CATL's near-term earnings trajectory. The Hungary investigation is widely viewed as manageable, while the capacity controls focus on future projects rather than expansions already under construction.

The debate that matters most is ESS demand. Investor concerns around the pace and sustainability of global energy storage growth have intensified in recent weeks, and the market is still searching for clarity. For now, the stock is caught in a classic feedback loop: weaker sentiment drives negative headlines, and negative headlines reinforce weaker sentiment.

The key question for investors is no longer why CATL has fallen. It is whether the market is correctly pricing a structural deterioration in fundamentals, or whether consensus pessimism has created the next contrarian opportunity in China.

BOTTOM LINE – 

The stock remains vulnerable. Buy-side numbers sit below consensus, sentiment is poor, and shorts are firmly in control. Further estimate cuts or negative headlines could keep the pressure on.

What matters, however, is who is not selling. Long-only investors appear largely committed, looking through the near-term noise and focusing on CATL's long-term technology, scale and profitability advantages. As long as they stay put, the bear case remains a positioning story more than a fundamental one.

The real warning sign would be if the stock rallies 20-30% and long-only holders start heading for the exits. That would signal a deeper crack in conviction.

Until then, the ball is in management's court. More aggressive buybacks and stronger investor engagement could quickly change the narrative.

For now, CATL looks less like a broken business and more like a stock trapped in a bearish feedback loop. The key question is whether fundamentals eventually force shorts to blink.
